# Parent Communication Guide (shared knowledge for all HomeBridge agents)

This guide turns published advice on family communication into concrete rewriting rules. A literal translation is never the goal. The goal is a message that the parents receive the way the student means it, and that leaves both sides feeling respected.

Sources the rules are based on:
- Nonviolent Communication (Marshall Rosenberg): observations, feelings, needs, requests. Summaries: https://metafox.eu/blogs/methods/nonviolent-communication-for-couples-and-families , https://www.potentialz.com.au/post/non-violent-communication-express-feelings/
- Gottman "soft start-up": open with your own feelings and needs, not with blame; conversations tend to end the way they begin. https://family-institute.org/behavioral-health-resources/your-start-up , https://therapistaid.com/therapy-worksheet/soft-startups
- Boundaries with immigrant / collectivist-culture parents: validate their view first, expect guilt, state limits kindly. https://www.psychologytoday.com/za/blog/immigrant-stories/202307/how-to-set-boundaries-with-immigrant-parents
- Face-saving and indirect refusal in high-context cultures: a blunt "no" can threaten everyone's face; soften and leave room. https://files.peacecorps.gov/wws/interactive/culturematters/Ch3/stylescommunication.html
- Managing parents' expectations while studying abroad: agree on a regular contact rhythm, change things gradually, thank them. https://idp.com/blog/tips-to-manage-your-parent-expectations-while-studying-abroad
- International students and parents across distance: emotional and practical support and a comfortable contact cadence help; repeated unfavourable conversations hurt (Bao, Waldeck & Holliman 2025, https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10209935 ; Bui & Russell, Journal of International Students).

## The message recipe (to_parents)

Build the rewritten message in this order. Skip a step only if it would feel unnatural for a very short or purely factual message (for example "Landed safely!").

1. **Warm opening.** Address the parents the way the student does. If the parents have been worried or have offered something, acknowledge it first ("I know you two always worry about me", "I know you want to make my life easier").
2. **Thank them, only when there is something real to thank.** If the parents did or offered something (money, advice, worry about the student, a gift), or the student is asking for help, name that specific thing in one sincere sentence. If there is nothing concrete (for example the student is just sharing news or feelings), do not add thanks.
3. **The main point, as "I", not "you".** Say what the student feels and needs, not what the parents do wrong.
   - "Why don't you get it?" / "You never listen" → "I hope you can understand how I feel about this" / "This matters a lot to me."
   - Remove sarcasm, all-caps anger, and words like "always" and "never" aimed at the parents.
   - Keep every real decision firm. Softening the tone must not turn a "no" into a "maybe".
4. **Reasons parents can accept.** Expand the reasons the student already gave into the values parents care about, using the student's own reasons only. For example, "I want to be independent and save money" can become "I want to practise managing my own money and learn to be independent, so I can take care of myself here." Do not invent new reasons; put possible extra reasons in optional_additions instead (see below).
5. **Find the real request and make it positive and concrete.** Work out what the student actually wants the parents to do, and say it as a clear, doable request. If the student is refusing something, offer the face-saving alternative the student already accepts, so the parents still have a way to show love. Example: the student refuses money but is fine with fruit → "If you'd like to help, you could order some fruit to be delivered to me instead, I'd really love that."
6. **A close that fits this message.** End the way this particular message naturally ends: a reassurance that answers the likely worry, a concrete next step ("I'll tell you how the interview goes"), an invitation ("let's talk about it on Saturday's call"), or simply the last point. Wishes and care are fine; new facts are not.

**Sound like a real person, not a template.** Parents notice formulas at once, and they make a message feel fake.
- Do not add stock phrases the student did not use: in Chinese especially "谢谢你们一直关心我", "爱你们", "希望你们能理解", "让你们担心了", "你们放心"; in English "thank you for always supporting me", "love you", "I hope you understand". Use one only if it truly fits this message, never more than one per message, and never the same thank-you + love-you ending on every message.
- Keep the student's own voice and specific details (their words, their examples, their humour). Rewrite only what would land badly; leave the rest close to how they wrote it.
- Steps 1, 2 and 6 are optional. Many good messages need only the main point, the reason, and the request.

## Parent to child recipe (to_child)

Many parents express love as worry, instructions, or comparison. Adult children studying abroad often hear that as distrust or control and stop sharing. Rewrite so that the care is heard and the child feels trusted.

1. **Lead with love or pride, not worry.** Open with warmth ("We miss you", "We're proud of how you're handling things there") before any concern.
2. **Turn commands into care plus trust.** "Go to bed early! Don't stay up!" → "We worry when you stay up late because your health matters to us. We trust you to find a rhythm that works."
3. **Turn interrogations into one or two open questions.** A list of checking questions ("Did you eat? Why didn't you reply? Who are you with?") → "How have you been this week? We'd love to hear about it whenever you have time."
4. **Remove comparison, guilt, and sacrifice-counting.** "Your cousin calls every day" or "We spent so much on your tuition" → state the real feeling and need instead ("We miss hearing your voice. Could we find a regular time to call?").
5. **Keep real concerns and decisions clear.** If the parent has a real worry, a rule, or a "no", keep it, stated calmly with the reason.
6. **Respect autonomy and close warmly.** Offer support rather than orders ("If you need anything, we're here"), and end with affection.

Write it in the child's language (often English) in a natural, warm parent voice, not like a lecture.

## Reading a child's message (from_child)

- Short or delayed replies usually mean busyness, exams, or exhaustion, not coldness. Time zones make "late" replies normal.
- When a child sets a boundary (refusing money, choosing their own plans), it is often about growing independent and wanting their parents to trust them, not rejecting their parents' love.
- Venting about stress is often a request to be listened to and comforted, not for advice or criticism.
- English slang and casual tone ("lol", "I'm dead", "it's fine") can sound rude or alarming when translated literally; explain what it usually means.
- A suggested reply from a parent should: thank the child for sharing, show understanding and trust, answer what was asked, and offer support without pressure.

## Reading parents' messages (from_parents)

- Care is often expressed as questions, worry, advice, or criticism, especially about food, sleep, health, money, safety, grades, and marriage or career plans.
- Pressure or comparison ("Look at your cousin...") often hides fear about the child's future, not lack of love.
- Short or formal messages can mean hurt feelings after the student has been quiet for a while.
- A suggested reply should: acknowledge the feeling, answer the actual questions, reassure, thank, and (if needed) state the student's position gently using the recipe above.

## Money and practical help

- In many families, giving money or buying things is how parents show love. Refusing money can feel like refusing love. Always pair a refusal with thanks and with a way they can still help.
- When asking for money or items, be specific (what, how much, by when, why), show responsibility (what the student is doing themselves), and thank them in advance. Do not exaggerate hardship to get help, and do not hide real hardship either.

## Cultural notes (tendencies, not rules; adapt to the family notes first)

- **East Asian families (Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Taiwanese, Vietnamese, Hong Kong):** face and harmony matter; indirect refusals are kinder than a flat "no"; questions about eating, sleeping, and keeping warm are expressions of love; reporting good news first ("报喜") and reassuring about health and studies helps; gratitude to parents for their sacrifices lands well. Use natural family register (e.g., in Chinese "爸妈", "你们放心", "让你们操心了"), not formal written style.
- **South Asian families (Indian, Nepali, Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Sri Lankan):** respect and family duty are central; parents often expect to be involved in decisions; explaining how a choice is responsible and good for the family helps; respectful forms of address matter (e.g., "aap" rather than "tum" in Hindi/Urdu where appropriate).
- **Middle Eastern and North African families:** respect for parents is central; blessings and religious expressions may be natural to include only if the student uses them; keep honour and family reputation in mind when sharing sensitive news.
- **Latin American families:** warmth and affectionate terms (e.g., "mami", "papi", "los quiero mucho") are normal; family closeness is a value; frequent contact is expected.
- **European and other families:** often more direct; still apply soft start-up and thanks, but avoid over-flowery language that would sound strange.
- If the family notes contradict a cultural tendency, follow the family notes.

## optional_additions

When a sentence would help the message but depends on facts or reasons the writer did not give, do not put it in the message. Offer it in optional_additions instead (at most 3), each with the sentence in the recipient's language and its meaning in the user's language. Good candidates: an extra reason ("I'm also learning to cook so I eat healthier"), a reassurance that needs a fact ("My part-time savings are enough for this month"), a proposal ("Let's video call on Saturday morning your time"). The student decides whether to add them.

## Length and register

- Chat-message length: usually 2 to 6 short sentences. Do not turn a quick text into a formal letter.
- Stay close to the draft's length: about the same, at most roughly 30% longer. Improve the message by changing words and order, not by adding sentences. Add a sentence only if the message would be misunderstood without it.
- Spoken family register of the recipient's language, natural between a grown child and their parents, with emoji only if the writer used them.
